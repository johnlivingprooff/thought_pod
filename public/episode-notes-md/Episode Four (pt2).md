# The Flesh — The Container

**Core theme:** Condition  
**Tone:** Reflective

---

I once found myself stuck on a question:

What does Paul mean when he says to _kill the flesh_?

The word _kill_ is violent. Serious. Final.

We only kill what we either hate—or what poses a danger that must be permanently removed.

So why would Paul use such language?

---

## Understanding the Flesh

In Romans 6:6, Paul explains that our old self was crucified with Christ so that the body of sin might be done away with—and that we would no longer serve sin.

Paul refers to the flesh as the **body of sin**.

Not sin itself—but the container that carries it.

The flesh is what was once good, but has been corrupted by what it holds. Something clean allowed something destructive to reshape it.

---

## The Problem of Corrupted Containers

This led me to think about content.

What does it mean for something to be _fresh_?

Is freshness simply the rebranding of something stale? Or is it true renewal?

When something old is repeatedly re-imaged as new, it slowly trains people to accept corruption as relevance. Over time, decay becomes familiar—and familiarity becomes normal.

This is why definition matters.

Freshness is not a twisted version of what is old. It is rejuvenation. Transformation. Newness that does not depend on preserving corruption.

---

## New Wine, New Wineskins

Jesus explains this clearly:

New wine cannot be poured into old wineskins.

New wine still expands. It grows. It ferments. But an old wineskin has already been stretched to its limit. It cannot grow further.

If new wine is poured into it, the skin bursts—and the wine is wasted.

The problem is not the wine.

The problem is the container.

---

## Why the Flesh Must Die

When Paul speaks of dying to the flesh, he is not describing behavior modification.

He is describing abandonment.

The old container—fully shaped by sin—cannot hold what is new, holy, or alive.

It must be destroyed.

Just as a bed infested with corruption must be burned and not repaired, a life shaped by the flesh cannot be patched—it must be remade.

This is not pretense.  
This is not imitation.  
This is transformation.

The death of the flesh is the end of the old container so that something truly new can exist.
